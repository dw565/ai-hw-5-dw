Reflection

Based on the final full run (the clean reset that started at $3,400) unless I say "earlier debugging". The ticket tabs, the Cash tab, the audit trail, and the working database are the evidence.

1. How did the agents perform on each ticket?

#101 (Bulldog tee). The team got the important part right: it spotted that Bulldog Print Co wouldn't ship anything while its $840 invoice was unpaid and overdue, so that bill had to be dealt with before any restock. The approval flow worked the way I wanted. Accounting prepared the $840 payment, Inventory prepared a separate $8 PO for the one tee, I approved both on the dashboard, and only then did cash move. The coordination was clunky, though. In the first pass, work bounced between Boss, Inventory, and Accounting enough that nine lookups were repeated (the invoice status, cash position, ticket, and vendor list each got pulled twice), and it took a second run before Boss would close the ticket. I also want to be clear about what "resolved" means here. Boss resolved it because the tee is paid for and on order, expected 2026-09-05 (the shop date plus Bulldog's 5-day lead time). Nobody received the tee or handed it to Tauhid. Our tools can't record a delivery or a sale, so "resolved" is really "everything the shop can do right now is done".

#102 (rent). The financial result was exactly right: $2,400 rent paid with my approval, the lease's next due date rolled forward to October 2, and the Cash tab reconciles. The execution was less reliable. It took two runs. In the second, Boss set the ticket to resolved twice, and then its final model call timed out, so the run was logged as a failure even though the database already said resolved. That was a useful lesson: a successful database update and a successful agent run aren't the same thing, and the dashboard has to show both honestly. (The board screenshot shows the timeout in the activity feed and no closing report from Boss.)

Speed (#101 and #102). Honestly, the agents took forever for simple tasks. The second #101 run took about four minutes even though it only needed to confirm two payments and update a draft. The second #102 run took nearly seven minutes before ending in that timeout. The logs don't tell me exactly where all of that time went. The #102 run ended in a recorded timeout, but the slow #101 run has no error recorded, so I can't pin it on one cause. Separately, during earlier debugging (not the final run), one model call hung for about ten minutes before we added a timeout. From a user's seat, watching a dashboard spin for minutes on a rent payment is frustrating, and it makes the system harder to trust even when the answer is right.

#103 (bulk hoodies). This was the strongest coordination of the three, done in a single run. Inventory confirmed 8 on hand against 20 requested. Accounting tested four discount levels (5%, 10%, 15%, 20%) and flagged that restocking 12 would cost $264 when only $152 was left. Boss made the call, and Customer Service wrote the reply without exposing our cash position. Offering the 8 available hoodies at 5% off ($55.10 each, $440.80 total) instead of spending money we didn't have was a sensible answer to the constraint. But it's a decision and a quote, not a completed sale: no inventory or cash changed, and we didn't fulfill the original request for 20.

2. How did Actual compare with Expected?

I left the Expected sections exactly as they were written before the runs, so the differences show up honestly.

  • #101. It followed the planned dependency (Boss → Inventory, then Inventory → Accounting about invoice 501), and Facilities stayed out as planned. The plan assumed the invoice itself ("Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S") already covered the tee, so paying it would release the reprint and no new PO would be needed. The agents didn't make that assumption. Once the vendor was unblocked, they raised an explicit $8 PO for one tee, which made the arrival date verifiable but added a purchase and a second run we didn't plan for. The plan also stopped at "awaiting approval"; the real run went on to resolve.
  • #102. The right specialists were involved (Facilities first, then Accounting, with Inventory left out), and cash landed exactly where the plan predicted after #101's invoice. But the plan had Facilities hand off to Accounting, and in practice Boss did that hand-off itself. Customer Service also drafted notes to the landlord, which the plan said weren't needed (I'd called that an optional deviation).
  • #103. The delegation chain matched the plan exactly (Inventory, then Accounting, then Customer Service), and as planned there was no PO and no cash change. It ended resolved instead of the expected blocked, because before the final run we changed the rules so Boss makes pricing decisions and humans only approve money. Available cash was $152, not the $160 the plan expected, because of #101's extra $8 tee PO.

3. What would have been simpler with one agent and tools?

My main takeaway is that separate roles can help, but we overcomplicated the straightforward work. The first #102 run used 17 model calls and about 55k tokens just to prepare one rent payment, and that included Accounting re-looking up rent details Facilities had already found. In #101, several lookups were repeated as the work bounced between agents. A single agent with the rent, cash, invoice, stock, and payment-request tools could likely skip most of those hand-offs and cut the waiting and token use. We didn't test a single-agent version, though, so I can't say exactly how many calls or minutes it would save. Across the whole final run it took five runs and roughly 282k tokens to finish three tickets, and that overhead is a big part of why it felt like waiting ages even on easy requests.

Separate agents earned their keep on #103. Checking stock, running the discount math, making the pricing call, and writing to the customer were genuinely different jobs, and keeping them apart meant the analysis (Accounting's four scenarios) stayed separate from the decision (Boss). It also meant the customer draft didn't leak internal numbers. Role-specific tool access was also nice: only Accounting can execute payments, Inventory can propose a PO but not pay it, and no agent can approve anything. To be fair, the parts that actually protect money, which are human approval and the "no negative balance" check, live inside the tools, so a single agent could have had the same guardrails.

4. Three new problems this team could handle with today's tools

There's no tool to create tickets, so these would be new requests handed to the team, the same way the three tickets were.

  1. Restock the Crest Mug (0 in stock). Inventory runs check_stock_shortfall and list_vendors and finds Elm City Gifts (mugs and small goods, 3-day lead, no open invoices). Accounting checks get_cash_position and prepares a propose_purchase_order, which waits for my approval before execute_payment moves any cash. At $3.50 a mug, 43 mugs would cost $150.50, almost all of the remaining $152. That's an affordability ceiling, not a recommended order size; Boss would still need to decide a sensible quantity. Until I approve it, it's only a proposal.
  2. Next month's rent on lease 1. Facilities runs get_rent_status (measured against desk.date_today, so it would show the October 2 payment as about a month away, not overdue), and Accounting checks get_cash_position. Accounting can prepare the $2,400 request with request_payment, but with $152 in checking it can't be paid: execute_payment would refuse, and the approval route would refuse before even recording an approval. The right outcome is Boss marking it blocked and flagging the shortfall, not pretending it's handled.
  3. Quote a bulk cap order. Inventory checks the 40 Yale Caps on hand with check_stock_shortfall. Accounting runs evaluate_discount at a few levels against the $6 cost and $22 list price. Boss picks the price, and Customer Service saves the reply with save_customer_draft. Like #103, this is a quote; no sale is recorded and no stock changes.

5. Three problems the current tools couldn't solve, and what we'd need

These are future extensions. The homework version stays drafts-only with no incoming revenue.

  1. Completing a sale (for example, actually selling #103's 8 hoodies). We'd need tools to record an order or sale, reserve and then deduct inventory, and record the incoming payment. Inventory would own the stock side and Accounting would own receivables and cash in, which is exactly the revenue this assignment deliberately doesn't model.
  2. Receiving the tee delivery. We'd need a tool to mark a PO as received and add the units to inventory, with Inventory responsible for verifying what arrived. An admin-only way to advance the simulated shop date would also help, since desk.date_today never moves, so 2026-09-05 never comes.
  3. Real customer messages and real vendor orders. Right now everything is a draft and a "placed" PO is just a database row. We'd need approved email and vendor-ordering integrations with delivery or order confirmations, with Customer Service owning outgoing messages and Inventory and Accounting owning orders and payments. Incoming emails would also need a ticket-creation tool so requests land on the board without someone typing them in.
